Hollow Creek

A hamlet on a river, late summer. Four people live here, and over a week ten more walk in from the west road with trades the place lacked. They build what they need, grow close, have children, and have good weeks and bad ones. Walk among them.

What happens there

A day in full

Mara bakes before dawn, Teo restores an old boat, Nadia works the market garden; a smith, a potter, a builder, a nurse, a teacher, a fisher and more take up their own work. Weekdays, a Saturday market, Sundays off; they eat, rest, visit and sleep in homes you can walk into.

Talk with a point

They go looking for each other to ask, offer, complain or make up; promises are kept or broken, and remembered.

A town that grows

A trade without a place gets one built, stage by stage: a smithy, a pottery, a net shed, a schoolhouse, a house by the river. Woodsheds and benches go up, storms damage them, and feet wear paths into the grass.

Lives that change

Good spells and bad patches, each with a cause; people who start seeing each other, move in together and have a baby; kindness, gossip and apologies; a chronicle of the town, a page a day.

A year that turns

A day here is a week of the year. Leaves turn and fall, snow lies on the roofs, blossom comes to the square and the garden grows again; people speak of the weather they have, and keep to the fire when the light goes early.

Whole lives

Children grow up, with their parents' faces mixed in their own, and take a trade; hair turns grey; people fall ill and mend, or do not. Nobody is spared: the dead are buried on the hill, mourned, and their work passes on.

A parish and its money

Beyond the houses you see is the rest of the parish. Wood, grain, fish, clay and ore are won from the land and made into boards, bread, bricks and iron; prices move with what there is; a meeting votes a rate, and in time a well, a constable, a school, a doctor.

Right and wrong

Short weight, a lie put about, a fence broken, a blow struck in a quarrel. What someone does when tempted is theirs to choose; a neighbour finds out, has it out with them, or brings it to the meeting. The year's page goes into the annals.

Dressed for the day

A coat goes on at the door when it is cold and comes off inside, the children's too; their best on a Sunday, a change when they feel like one; the apron is for work. Newcomers come with hair and a height of their own, and sometimes as a whole family, to a house with a bed for each. Snow lies on the fences and the benches.

Who runs the place

A reeve and a clerk, then a council, a mayor, magistrates and police, each somebody by name. Some want the chair, some want a thing done since the winter that took a brother, some say what the room wants to hear; it changes them to win, to lose, and to stay too long. One person steals once in a hard year and never again; another comes to live by it. The constable walks the round at dusk with a lantern; the council sits at the café's tables of an evening.

A fiddle on the green

On a Saturday in the warm months whoever plays gets up a dance: a fiddle and a drum, couples going round, the rest looking on. Feasts are danced. Two throw a ball on the green, and once the meeting has marked out a pitch on the south meadow, two more kick one about.

The minds

In this web edition the townspeople think by rules: their routines, templated talk and simple judgements. On the computer it was made on, a local language model thinks for each of them: their plans, every line they say, what they make of a conversation, their reflections at night and the chronicle's pages.

You can have your own LM Studio think for them, in Chrome or Firefox: turn on CORS in LM Studio's server settings, click the badge at the top of the town (Connect LM Studio), press Connect, and choose Allow when the browser asks whether the page may reach apps on your computer. From then on it connects by itself on every visit. Everything stays on your machine. (Safari doesn't let a web page reach LM Studio this way.)
